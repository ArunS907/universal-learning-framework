# Subject adaptation examples

The examples below show how to adapt the Universal Learning Framework without changing its nine-step order.

## Mathematics — derivatives
- Intuition: instantaneous rate of change.
- First principles: average rate of change over a shrinking interval.
- Science: velocity as the derivative of position.
- Applied: marginal cost or revenue.
- Daily life: speedometer reading as an instantaneous rate.
- Visualization: tangent line moving along a curve.
- Formalism: derive the limit definition.
- Worked example: differentiate a polynomial step by step.
- Reflection: what does the derivative tell us at a particular point?

## Programming — recursion
- Intuition: a function solves a problem by solving a smaller version of it.
- First principles: repeated structure and a stopping condition.
- Science: branching structures as a limited analogy, with caveats.
- Applied: traversing a file tree.
- Daily life: nested boxes.
- Visualization: call stack and shrinking subproblems.
- Formalism: base case plus recursive case.
- Worked example: factorial or tree traversal.
- Reflection: why does recursion need a base case?

## Biology — natural selection
- Intuition: inherited traits that improve reproductive success can become more common.
- First principles: variation, inheritance, and differences in reproductive success.
- Science: antibiotic resistance, distinguishing selection from an individual organism adapting by choice.
- Applied: pest management or conservation.
- Daily life: use analogies carefully and state their limits.
- Visualization: trait-frequency change across generations.
- Formalism: a simple population-frequency model with assumptions.
- Worked example: track trait frequency in a simplified population.
- Reflection: why does selection act on existing variation rather than plan ahead?

## Finance — compound interest
- Intuition: returns can themselves earn returns.
- First principles: each period's balance becomes the next period's starting balance.
- Science: exponential growth as a mathematical pattern, not a claim that money behaves like an unconstrained physical population.
- Applied: savings growth or loan balance.
- Daily life: recurring deposits and accumulated interest.
- Visualization: compare simple and compound growth.
- Formalism: derive the periodic compounding formula.
- Worked example: calculate a balance and interpret assumptions.
- Reflection: why do time and rate interact?
